## What counts as عرنجية

Arabic on the surface and foreign underneath: Arabic words and correct case
endings, but the sentence frame, the word's sense, the pairing of words or
the image follows English or French (ص 8، 33). Loanwords and the famous
calques (لعب دورًا، تمّ إرسال، تغذية راجعة) are the visible layer. The book
places most of the problem in construction, word sense and style, so a text
free of the famous calques is not clean for that reason (ص 8، 45، 132).

Look at polished prose as closely as sloppy prose. Teachers, literary
scholars, historians and authors of usage guides write it, original
(untranslated) Arabic carries the same patterns as translations, and the
author's random samples from a literary magazine were full of it (ص 8، 24،
28، 79، 176). Calques concentrate in plain everyday prose: news, reports,
instructions, book descriptions (ص 200).

## Judging a borderline case

1. **Grammatical is not the test.** Grammar (نحو) means following how
   Arabs build speech, not only getting the endings right. «حدث الأمر
   خارجًا من الأزرق» parses perfectly and every reader rejects it; a
   familiar phrase that differs from it only in being familiar has
   nothing else defending it. When a construction prompts a grammar debate
   (الأفعل agreement, رئيسي or رئيس), first ask whether Arabic would use a
   different structure altogether. ص 36–37، 47، 117، 136
2. **Familiarity and justification are not evidence.** Readers stop
   noticing calques they meet constantly, so "it sounds natural" from a
   reader raised on translated prose doesn't clear one. None of these
   clears a phrase either: an academy ruling (ص 50), a derivation found
   after the fact (ص 39، 70، 113–115), "no grammarian forbids it" (ص 48), a
   famous writer who used it (ص 79), calling it "modern standard Arabic"
   (ص 23), or a rare old instance (ص 129–131، 169). Ask instead whether the
   usage became common through translation and whether it displaced a
   native word. If both, flag it.
3. **Provenance, not novelty.** New expressions from an Arabic faculty are
   sound, and resembling a European phrase is not enough to flag. Coinages
   for genuinely new concepts (technical terms) are fine. The fault is a
   foreign word or sense for a meaning Arabic already expressed. Don't call
   a word foreign on a hunch: the evidence is a sense and collocations that
   match the English word and an absence before the translation era.
   ص 16، 38، 40، 72، 126
4. **Find the native form it displaced.** A calque rarely fills a gap; it
   replaces a native construction and leaves it dead in writing
   (يبدو كما لو ← كأنّ، فقد الاهتمام ← زهد). Offer that form. If everyday
   speech still uses it (ثوب صوف، نمت ثلاث ساعات، قسا قلبه، ما عندنا خبر،
   أبطأ علينا), the rewrite is safe. ص 41، 44، 114، 185
5. **Helper words stand in for derived forms.** English has no تصغير,
   intensive, تفاعل, causative stem, استفعل, inflected comparative,
   predicate participle, نعت سببي or مفعول مطلق. عرنجية fills each gap with
   a helper word: جعل، أكثر، أصبح، بعضهم بعضًا، كثيرًا، بشكل، يبدو، صغير.
   ص 67، 156
6. **Judge the frame, not one word.** Swapping the most visible word keeps
   the calque and makes it harder to spot: لعب ← أدّى دورًا، كـ ← بوصفه،
   حرفيًّا ← فعليًّا، بكلمات أخرى ← بعبارة أخرى، لا يجب ← يجب ألّا، استدار
   بعيدًا ← التفت بعيدًا. List such variants under the same entry and flag
   the frame. ص 132، 134، 141، 147، 177، 205
7. **Weigh density.** Translation also thins Arabic without producing a
   single wrong sentence: a native form dies (الإماتة), only the option that
   matches English survives (الاستحياء), or that option crowds out the rest
   (التغليب). A single 🟡 item is a suggestion. A passage where nearly every
   choice mirrors English (هناك، بعض، سوف، فقط، كان + صفة، أيّ، بشكل)
   deserves a note on the pattern, and a passage dense with calques
   deserves a full rewrite from its meaning, not a list of patches. Calques
   cluster: one sentence often holds two or three, and a calque from one
   domain (sex, family, opinion) is a reason to check its neighbors.
   ص 56، 72، 74، 80، 118، 182
8. **Long and vague against short and native.** A heavy multi-word
   expression where a short native word exists (العمل الجنسي for الجماع،
   ذهب بعيدًا عنه for انتبذ) is a sign of a calque. See the lookup table
   below. ص 73، 123–125
9. **Check the sense against the root.** When a native word is used in a
   sense its own meaning doesn't support (سلبي for bad, حلم for hope،
   فشل for could not), look for the English word whose range it copies.
   Calqued senses also make readers misread older texts: صفة سلبية in
   الرازي is a negated attribute, كتاب بسيط is a long book, احترف كذا means
   he earned his living by it, خُطّة in a hadith is a matter, not a plan.
   Don't flag these words inside a classical quotation. ص 69، 71، 93،
   105، 129
10. **Severity.** Give 🔴 when the book treats the pattern as plainly
    foreign and the native wording is just as clear. Being widespread does
    not lower a calque to 🟡. Give 🟡 when the book is less firm, when the
    native form is heavier or would surprise readers, or when the pattern
    appears only in the book's back-translations without its own verdict.
    Never give 🔴 on a guess.

## Tests for a sentence no entry covers

The references list the book's examples, and the book says the phrase
calques far outnumber what it lists (ص 112). For a suspect phrase with no
entry, run these tests.

- **Back-translation.** Gloss the sentence into English word for word. If
  the result is already fluent, idiomatic English, with the same word
  order and each Arabic word mapping to the same part of speech (noun to
  noun, verb to verb), the frame is English. Classical prose fails this
  test: a translator must restructure it. If the phrase won't go into
  English literally, try French before clearing it (الأوساط = dans les
  milieux). ص 137، 167–168، 179، 182، 201
- **Colloquial.** Ask how a speaker would say it in their own dialect. The
  dialect often keeps the native construction (تلقاه في مكة، ما في
  المحفظة ريال، فلان تغيّر عليّ، ما فهم أحد إلا …), while the عرنجي version
  matches the English. Closeness to dialect is not a sign of weak style.
  ص 44، 63، 156–157، 162–163
- **Five shifts.** Compare the sentence with how classical prose would put
  the same meaning, and look for: a verb turned into a noun or a noun into
  a verb; changed word order; a word added to spell out what Arabic leaves
  implicit (الآخرين، شخص، هناك، يمكن، جيدًا) or dropped because English
  doesn't need it; a word in a sense Arabs never gave it; the whole
  sentence reshaped to an English pattern. ص 140
- **Classical parallel.** Find a classical sentence that says the same
  thing and model the rewrite on it. When a modern text retells a
  classical source, set the two side by side. ص 79، 185

## Rewriting

1. **Rebuild from meaning; don't patch words.** Translated prose stays
   captive to the source order. Rebuild the sentence: verb first where
   natural, the main event as a verb with circumstances as حال, links with
   و and ف, and the verb the noun phrase was standing in for. Check that
   the fix removes the foreign construction, not just an error inside it
   (يجب ألّا is logical and still foreign). ص 27، 132، 182، 205
2. **Native, not archaic.** Aim at plain natural narrative Arabic, the kind
   in hadith, أخبار and early prose, not ornate rhetoric or rare dictionary
   words. A rewrite can sound unfamiliar to a modern reader without being
   difficult, but it should never be obscure. ص 43، 199–200
3. **Revive the specific word.** A calqued sense usually displaced several
   native words (محاسن، مساوئ، معايب، مثالب، آفات). Pick the one that fits
   the context instead of one generic substitute, and sometimes no noun is
   needed at all (نشاطهم الأدبي ← اشتغالهم بالأدب). ص 70، 94، 114
4. **Reach for constructions English lacks:** النعت السببي (قومٌ كثيرةٌ
   أفضالُهم), الصفة المشبهة + إضافة (رجلٌ حادُّ الأنف), تفاعل (تعاونوا),
   causative stems (أضحكه), استفعل for "I found it X" (استصعبته), أفعل +
   مضاف إليه (أحرص الناس), صيغ المبالغة (كذوب), the predicate participle
   (أنا سائلك), the bare ظرف for a duration (أقام يومين), tanween for the
   indefinite (سقاه ماءً), من التبعيضية (من خير الولاة), إنما and ما … إلا
   for restriction, ما … أحد إلا for "whoever … will", إذا for a
   condition and لمّا for a past sequence, كان + مضارع for a past habit,
   a fronted predicate for "there is" (في المحفظة ريال), and emphasis built
   into the sentence (القسم، إنّ، اللام، النون، المفعول المطلق). ص 55–67،
   134، 146–166، 205
5. **Explain old words with native synonyms.** When glossing a classical
   word, don't explain it with the calque (الإنعاظ ← الانتصاب). ص 76
6. **Reference writers.** Books written before about 1250 AH are safe
   models; writers born before about 1300 AH (c. 1883) are largely free of
   the style. A construction attested there leans native; one attested only
   later leans calque. Modern dictionaries and press usage are weaker
   evidence. ص 34، 200
7. **Expect pushback.** A reader raised on calques may feel the native
   rewrite "doesn't mean the same" (خير معايش الناس for "the best
   lifestyle"). That reaction is a symptom, not a reason to drop the
   rewrite. Keep it and explain in one line. ص 115–116، 157
8. **When translating from English:** drop the English word, read its
   dictionary definition, and phrase that meaning in Arabic (self-respect
   ← أنفة، عزّة نفس). To see how classical Arabic said it, search English
   translations of the Qur'an and hadith, or a parallel corpus such as
   الرصائف (rasaif.com), for the English word and read the Arabic it
   renders. Treat those English translations as a guide to construction,
   not to meaning: they can be wrong. ص 204، 207، 213–217
9. **If the user asks how to improve their own ear:** taste follows what
   you read most. Build it on pre-translation texts first (Qur'an, hadith,
   plain classical narrative such as البخلاء and عيون الأخبار), read them
   aloud, listen to recordings of them, imitate their sentences, and only
   then read modern stylists. Rules learned apart from texts don't yield
   natural sentences. ص 29، 40، 80، 196–203

## Do not flag

These look suspicious to a modern ear, or overlap with a cue in the
references, but are sound Arabic.

| Expression | Why it's sound | ص |
|---|---|---|
| زوجه / زوجك for a wife (and زوجة too) | Qur'anic (أمسك عليك زوجك). Both are sound, and زوج is the more eloquent | 16، 25 |
| النعت السببي: ثمراتٍ مختلفًا ألوانُها، القرية الظالمِ أهلُها | Qur'an, hadith, 19th-century prose and living speech. Never call it stilted | 44، 56–57 |
| الصفة المشبهة مضافة إلى فاعلها: حادّ الأنف، عظيم المنظر | Native. It became rare only because English lacks it | 57 |
| صيغ المبالغة and اسم الفاعل as predicate: حلّاف، كذوب، أنا سائلك | Native and alive in speech. The "forced" feel comes from English habits | 66 |
| قلّ يومٌ إلا وهو … | Native negative-restrictive idiom; a good rewrite for «بالكاد يمرّ يوم دون أن» | 193 |
| Single ضمير الفصل: صاحب السيارة هو خالد | Acceptable; SYN-028 targets يكون as a copula. Flag هو only when every sentence carries it | 146 |
